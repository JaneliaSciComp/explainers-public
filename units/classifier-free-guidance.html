Classifier-free guidance

Upper undergraduate 7 min read

The idea in one paragraph

Classifier-free guidance makes a conditional generative model follow its condition more strongly. At each generation step, the network makes two predictions: one with the condition and one without. The guided prediction starts from the one without and moves a chosen scale s times the difference between them. A scale of 1 gives plain conditional generation. Above 1, it overshoots the conditional prediction, so samples show the condition more clearly but vary less. One network can make both predictions, because training sometimes hides the condition.

The convention on this page

Predictions are velocities, as in flow matching, with time t from 0 (pure noise) to 1 (data). Diffusion models apply the same formula to their noise predictions.

Two predictions, then a step past the conditional one

Write v(x, t, c) for the velocity the network predicts with the condition c, and v(x, t, ∅) for its prediction with no condition. The symbol ∅ stands for a special "no condition" input. The guided velocity is

vguided = v(x, t, ∅) + s · ( v(x, t, c) − v(x, t, ∅) ).

  • s = 0 gives the unconditional prediction.
  • s = 1 gives the conditional prediction.
  • s between 0 and 1 lands between the two.
  • s above 1 extrapolates: it goes past the conditional prediction, further in the direction that leads from "no condition" to "condition".

Generation then follows vguided with Euler steps, exactly as it would follow the plain prediction.

Worked example: one guided velocity

At some point and time, the network predicts v(x, t, ∅) = (1, 0) and v(x, t, c) = (2, 1).

  1. The difference is (2, 1) − (1, 0) = (1, 1).
  2. With s = 1: (1, 0) + 1 × (1, 1) = (2, 1), the conditional prediction.
  3. With s = 3: (1, 0) + 3 × (1, 1) = (4, 3), twice as far again beyond it.

Your turn. With the same two predictions, what are the guided velocities for s = 2 and s = 0.5?

Answer

s = 2: (1, 0) + 2 × (1, 1) = (3, 2). s = 0.5: (1, 0) + 0.5 × (1, 1) = (1.5, 0.5), halfway between the two predictions.

Training hides the condition now and then, so one network learns both

Guidance needs both predictions from the same network. Training gets them with condition dropout:

  1. For each training example, draw a random number.
  2. With a small probability, often 10%, replace the example's condition with the "no condition" input ∅.
  3. Train exactly as usual on the result.

The network then learns the conditional velocity from examples that keep their condition. It learns the unconditional velocity from examples whose condition was hidden. Nothing else in training changes. At generation time, each step runs the network twice, once with c and once with ∅, often as one batch of two.

The name comes from history. An earlier method, classifier guidance, steered a diffusion model with a separate classifier trained on noisy examples. Classifier-free guidance gets the same kind of steering from the generative network alone, with no classifier.

On a toy, a larger scale pushes samples away from the other class

Take two classes of 2D points. Class A is a bell curve around (−1, 0), class B one around (1, 0), each with spread 0.5. Without a condition, the data mix both classes equally. For this toy, the exact flow-matching velocities can be computed, so they stand in for a trained network. The condition is "class A".

What you should find

At s = 0 the samples cover both classes. At s = 1 they match class A. Above 1 they move away from class B, past A's own center, and their average passes −1.5 near s = 3. Their left-to-right spread shrinks, but their top-to-bottom spread stays the same, because the classes do not differ from top to bottom.

The program below runs the same toy with 4,000 samples and 100 Euler steps.

guidance_toy.py

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
CENTERS = {"A": np.array([-1.0, 0.0]), "B": np.array([1.0, 0.0])}
SPREAD = 0.5                                  # each class: a bell curve with this spread

def velocity(x, t, classes):
    """Exact flow-matching velocity when the data are an equal mix of the listed
    classes. It stands in for a trained network on this toy."""
    s2 = t**2 * SPREAD**2 + (1 - t)**2        # squared spread of x_t within a class
    k = (t * SPREAD**2 - (1 - t)) / s2
    vs = [CENTERS[c] + k * (x - t * CENTERS[c]) for c in classes]
    logw = np.stack([-((x - t * CENTERS[c])**2).sum(1) / (2 * s2) for c in classes], 1)
    w = np.exp(logw - logw.max(1, keepdims=True))
    w /= w.sum(1, keepdims=True)              # how likely each class is, given x
    return sum(w[:, [i]] * v for i, v in enumerate(vs))

def generate(scale, n=4000, steps=100):
    x = rng.standard_normal((n, 2))           # noise at t = 0
    for k in range(steps):
        t = k / steps
        v_u = velocity(x, t, ["A", "B"])      # without the condition
        v_c = velocity(x, t, ["A"])           # with the condition "class A"
        x = x + (v_u + scale * (v_c - v_u)) / steps
    return x

for scale in [0, 1, 3]:
    x = generate(scale)
    print(f"scale {scale}: mean x {x[:, 0].mean():+.2f}, "
          f"spread x {x[:, 0].std():.2f}, spread y {x[:, 1].std():.2f}")

scale 0: mean x -0.01, spread x 1.12, spread y 0.49
scale 1: mean x -0.99, spread x 0.49, spread y 0.49
scale 3: mean x -1.53, spread x 0.30, spread y 0.49

Scale 1 reproduces class A: mean −0.99, spread 0.49. Scale 3 pushes the samples past A's center, away from B, and narrows them from left to right. In real models, a scale above 1 often makes samples match their condition more clearly, at the cost of variety. Too large a scale distorts them.

Why it works: the difference points toward "more like the condition"

The difference v(x, t, c) − v(x, t, ∅) is the part of the velocity that the condition adds. Scaling it up exaggerates what makes examples of c differ from examples in general. In the toy, A differs from the mix only from left to right, so guidance acts only from left to right.

In terms of probability, guidance with scale s changes the distribution at each noise level. It starts from the unconditional distribution p(x) and weights each point x by p(c | x)s. Here p(c | x) is the probability that a classifier of noisy inputs gives to c. At s = 1 the result, p(x) · p(c | x), is proportional to the conditional distribution p(x | c) by Bayes' rule. A larger s favors points that the classifier is surer about.

An intuition, not an exact law

The probability statement holds at each noise level separately. The sampler chains all the levels, and the chain does not in general draw exactly from the tilted distribution of the data. Papers also write the formula differently. The original paper uses (1 + w) × conditional − w × unconditional, so its w equals s − 1 here, and its w = 0 is plain conditional generation.

Check yourself

  1. The unconditional prediction is (0, 2) and the conditional one is (1, 1). What is the guided velocity with s = 4?

    Answer

    The difference is (1, −1), so (0, 2) + 4 × (1, −1) = (4, −2).

  2. A model was trained without condition dropout. Why can you not use classifier-free guidance with it?

    Answer

    The network never saw the "no condition" input, so its unconditional prediction was never trained. Guidance needs that prediction. You would need a second, unconditional model.

  3. Why do samples vary less as the scale grows above 1?

    Answer

    Guidance pushes every sample further in the direction that separates the condition from the rest. That squeezes the samples toward the most clearly "condition-like" region. In the toy, the left-to-right spread falls from 0.49 at s = 1 to 0.30 at s = 3.

  4. A generator takes 30 Euler steps with guidance. How many network runs does one sample need?

    Answer

    60: two runs per step, one with the condition and one without.
