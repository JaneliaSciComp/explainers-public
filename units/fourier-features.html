Fourier features: positions as waves

Advanced

Read these first if they are new to you

The idea in one paragraph

A network that reads a raw coordinate, such as x = 0.30, finds it hard to treat nearby positions very differently. Fourier features replace the coordinate by the sine and cosine of it at several frequencies. Low frequencies say roughly where the position is. High frequencies change fast, so they tell nearby positions apart. A linear layer then turns these features into the position's embedding. Think of a clock: the hour hand gives the rough time, and the minute and second hands give the detail.

A raw coordinate makes nearby positions hard to tell apart

Suppose a shape has an edge at x = 0.31: positions just left of it are inside, positions just right of it are outside. A network must give opposite answers for x = 0.30 and x = 0.32. Yet the two inputs differ by only 0.02. The first layer computes weighted sums of x, and those sums differ by only 0.02 times a weight. To make the two sums very different, the layer needs very large weights.

In practice, networks trained on raw coordinates learn the smooth, slowly changing parts of a function first, and sharp detail very slowly. This tendency is called spectral bias. Fourier features work around it by handing the network inputs that already change fast.

Each frequency turns a position into a sine and a cosine

Pick a frequency f: the number of full waves per unit of position. For a position x, compute the pair sin(2πfx) and cos(2πfx), as in the sine waves unit. The factor 2π makes f count full turns: as x runs from 0 to 1, the pair goes around a circle f times. With k frequencies, the Fourier features of x are 2k numbers. We write them as φ(x), with the Greek letter phi:

φ(x) = [ sin(2πf1x), cos(2πf1x), …, sin(2πfkx), cos(2πfkx) ]

The name honors Joseph Fourier, who showed that sums of sines and cosines can build almost any repeating curve. A layer that reads φ(x) can mix these waves with its weights, so fast-changing functions of x become easy to make.

  1. For f = 4 and x = 0.30, the angle is 2π × 4 × 0.30 = 7.540 radians. Its sine is 0.951 and its cosine is 0.309.
  2. The "gap" is the straight-line distance between the two (sin, cos) pairs. For f = 4 it is √((0.951 − 0.982)2 + (0.309 + 0.187)2) = 0.497.
  3. Put the three pairs together into six features per position. The distance between the two feature vectors is √(0.1262 + 0.4972 + 1.6892) = 1.765.

The raw positions differ by 0.02. Their Fourier features differ by 1.765, about 88 times as much. The frequency-16 pair does most of that work.

One high frequency alone repeats itself

Predict first. Frequency 16 separates the two positions better than frequency 4. Will frequency 50 separate them better still?

So a single frequency is ambiguous: any two positions a whole number of waves apart look the same to it. This is why Fourier features use several frequencies, from low to high. The low frequencies tell distant positions apart without repeating. The high frequencies tell close positions apart. Together they pin down the position, like the hands of a clock.

Models choose the frequencies in one of three common ways

ChoiceFrequenciesWhere you meet it
Powers of two1, 2, 4, …, 2k−1 waves across the range of the dataNeural fields that map a 3D position to a color or density
A geometric spreadwavelengths from 2π up to 10,000 × 2πThe original transformer's encoding of word positions; the encoding of time in diffusion and flow models
Randomdrawn from a bell curve whose spread you chooseThe 2020 study of Fourier features for networks that read coordinates (Tancik and others)

The lowest frequency should not repeat inside the range of the data. With f = 1 as the lowest, positions 0 and 1 get the same features, so the data should lie in [0, 1). The highest frequency sets the finest detail the network can easily make.

A 3D point p = (x, y, z) works the same way. Either encode each coordinate separately, or choose frequency vectors b and use sin(2π b · p) and cos(2π b · p), where · is the dot product. A model that must know the time t of a process, such as a step in a generative model, encodes t the same way (see time conditioning).

fourier_features.py

import numpy as np

def fourier_features(x, freqs):
    angles = 2 * np.pi * np.outer(x, freqs)    # one row per position
    return np.concatenate([np.sin(angles), np.cos(angles)], axis=1)

freqs = np.array([1.0, 4.0, 16.0])
F = fourier_features(np.array([0.30, 0.32]), freqs)
print(np.round(F, 3))                          # sines first, then cosines
print(round(np.linalg.norm(F[0] - F[1]), 3))   # gap between the feature vectors

Simplifications on this page

Spectral bias is a tendency that experiments and theory for very wide networks support, not a law. A network on raw coordinates can learn fine detail; it is just slow. Frequencies that are too high also cause trouble: the network can fit noise in the training data and produce a speckled result, so the frequency range is a setting to tune. Some models also skip fixed waves and learn a table of position embeddings instead.

Check yourself

  1. A model encodes each coordinate of a 3D point with 8 frequencies, sine and cosine. How many features does one point get?

    Answer

    3 coordinates × 8 frequencies × 2 (sine and cosine) = 48 features. Some models also keep the 3 raw coordinates, for 51.

  2. Work a similar problem. Positions 0.1 and 0.6 are encoded with the single frequency 2. What are their features? Which of the frequencies 1 or 4 would tell them apart?

    Answer

    At f = 2 they are 2 × 0.5 = 1 full wave apart, so both get sin = 0.951 and cos = 0.309. At f = 4 they are 2 full waves apart and still look the same. At f = 1 they are half a wave apart: (0.588, 0.809) against (−0.588, −0.809), opposite points of the circle, with the largest possible gap of 2.

  3. Why use both a sine and a cosine at each frequency? Hint: compare positions 0.1 and 0.4 at frequency 1.

    Answer

    Both positions have sin(2πx) = 0.588, so the sine alone cannot tell them apart. Their cosines are 0.809 and −0.809. Together, the pair is a point on a circle, which fixes where in the wave the position sits.
