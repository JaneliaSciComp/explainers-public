The idea in one paragraph

Data often arrive at very different positions and sizes: one list in centimeters, another in kilometers; one shape near the origin, another far away and ten times larger. Normalizing makes them comparable in two steps. First subtract the center, so the data sit around zero. Then divide by a measure of size, so the data have size one. Keep the two numbers you used, because they let you undo the change later.

Subtract the mean, then divide by the spread

Take five heights in centimeters: 150, 160, 170, 180 and 190. Their mean is 170 and their standard deviation is about 14.14, computed as in the mean-and-spread unit. Subtract the mean from each height, then divide by the standard deviation. The result for each value is its z-score: how many standard deviations it lies above or below the mean.

z = (value − mean) / standard deviation

The new list has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, whatever the units were. A height of 190 cm becomes 1.41, and so would a weight or a price that sat the same number of standard deviations above its own mean. This step is called standardizing.

Worked example
  1. Mean: (150 + 160 + 170 + 180 + 190) / 5 = 170.
  2. Deviations: −20, −10, 0, 10, 20. Squares: 400, 100, 0, 100, 400. Their mean is 1000 / 5 = 200, so the standard deviation is √200 ≈ 14.14.
  3. Divide each deviation by 14.14: −1.41, −0.71, 0, 0.71, 1.41.

Your turn. A list has mean 50 and standard deviation 5. What are the z-scores of 60 and 47.5? What value has a z-score of −2?

Answer

(60 − 50) / 5 = 2 and (47.5 − 50) / 5 = −0.5. To undo, multiply and add back: −2 × 5 + 50 = 40.

For a shape, move its center to the origin and scale it to size one

A shape made of points is a list of vectors. To normalize it, first find its center: the mean point, whose x is the mean of all the x's and whose y is the mean of all the y's. Subtract the center from every point; this is called centering. Then pick one number for the shape's size, such as the distance from the center to the farthest point, and divide every point by it. Afterwards every point lies within distance 1 of the origin.

Worked example

The triangle with corners (2, 2), (4, 2) and (3, 5).

  1. Center: ((2 + 4 + 3) / 3, (2 + 2 + 5) / 3) = (3, 3).
  2. Subtract it: (−1, −1), (1, −1), (0, 2).
  3. Distances from the origin: √2 ≈ 1.41, √2 ≈ 1.41, and 2. The largest is 2.
  4. Divide by 2: (−0.5, −0.5), (0.5, −0.5), (0, 1).

The house's peak, the corner farthest from its center, is the one that touches the circle.

This is a simplification

There is more than one choice for "center" and "size." The size can be the farthest distance, the standard deviation, or the width of a bounding box; each gives a different scale. Pick one and use it everywhere. Also, a model trained on normalized data expects new data normalized with the same recipe, and its answers come back in normalized units. Store the center and size for each example so you can turn the answers back into the original units.

norm.py: z-scores for a list, and a centered and scaled shape

import numpy as np

heights = np.array([150, 160, 170, 180, 190])
z = (heights - heights.mean()) / heights.std()
print(np.round(z, 2))

shape = np.array([[2.0, 2.0], [4.0, 2.0], [3.0, 5.0]])  # one point per row
center = shape.mean(axis=0)
centered = shape - center
size = np.linalg.norm(centered, axis=1).max()     # farthest point from the center
print(center, size)
print(centered / size)

[3. 3.] 2.0

axis=0 takes the mean down each column, giving one mean per coordinate. axis=1 takes each point's length across its row.

Check yourself

  1. Scores have mean 170 and standard deviation 15. What is the z-score of 185?

    Answer

    (185 − 170) / 15 = 1: one standard deviation above the mean.

  2. A shape was normalized with center (10, 4) and size 5. A program returns the normalized point (0.2, −1). Where is that point in the original units?

    Answer

    Multiply by the size, then add the center: (0.2 × 5 + 10, −1 × 5 + 4) = (11, −1).

  3. You double every point of a shape and then normalize it. How does the result compare with normalizing the original shape?

    Answer

    It is the same. Doubling doubles the center and the size, and the division cancels it. That is the point of normalizing: size and position no longer matter.
